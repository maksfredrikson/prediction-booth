About this research

Ornament is not decoration.

Many people mistake ornament for decoration: a border, a pattern to fill a surface. In Belarusian villages it was a way of saying something. A ritual towel was not for drying hands. It was woven for a wedding, a feast day, a guest met with bread and salt, and its red pattern carried what a household hoped would come. Nobody wrote the wish down. It was made row by row, in a language of dots that everyone could see and each person read in their own way.

The official story says that in 1917 Matrona Markevich wove such a towel, and that in 1951 her pattern was placed on the flag of Soviet Belarus under the name “the rising sun”. A historian has since argued that no weaver ever used that name, and that the story was shaped by the professor who proposed the pattern, her brother. Whoever is right, a pattern made for one family ended up standing for a country. Nobody predicted that.

Three questions I ask of any pattern

The meaning of a pattern is made between the pattern and whoever reads it. So I ask three things: what is the data, who reads it, and how do people take part? Here they are, asked three times.

The Prediction Booth turns the towel around. A towel says what you hope for. Here you say what you expect: how you see the Netherlands today, and where you think it is heading. In the zine the marks were placed for the reader. Here you place them yourself. One card is one view. Together the cards show how differently the same country looks from where each of us stands.

Why this matters to people who design

Designers ship more and more interfaces that read data for people: a score, a summary, a generated scenario. Every such reading carries someone's idea of what counts. What stays with the person is their answer to the reading: placing the mark, disagreeing with it. That place can be designed.

I am a designer, and this is part of my master's research into how patterns come to carry meaning, and who gets to say what they mean.

How your text is made

Every step on every question has one sentence, written in advance. Your scenario text is those sentences put together by three rules.

  1. Today. The four steps you marked for today are joined into one paragraph: “You see the Netherlands today as a country where …”.
  2. Prediction. Only the questions where your prediction differs from today go into the second paragraph, with how soon. If nothing moved, it says so.
  3. Price. The question you moved farthest adds one line on what that move costs. Two questions tied for farthest add a line each.

What the booth keeps

  • Your marks: today, prediction, and the time frame.
  • Your one line of reasoning.
  • Your consent answer: whether your marks and words may be shown in the research publication.
  • If you tip it: how long you have lived in the Netherlands.

The booth keeps your marks and your words, never your name. On this website your answer stays in the browser you gave it in. Nothing is sent anywhere.
