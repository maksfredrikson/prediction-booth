About this research

Ornament is not decoration.

We often mistake ornament for decoration. A Belarusian ornamented towel carried what one hoped would come, in a shared language of pattern. Now this language is data.

The Prediction Booth uses the same logic to make predictions visible. You answer four questions about the Netherlands, once for today and once for where you think it is heading. The booth prints a receipt of what you gave, with a short prediction based on your answers. Then you mark your answers with stickers on a card with a printed grid, and your prediction becomes a pattern.

The card is yours to keep, and to show or not. It is a device for dialogue: a prediction made visible. Over the day the cards add up to one picture of how differently the same country looks to the people in the room.

The booth is a speculation: what if the intelligence worth asking is the embodied kind, yours? Here the prediction comes from people, not from a model, and the responsibility for it stays with them.

A thing that cares about your data tells you what it took. The booth keeps your answers and never your name.

Three questions I ask of any pattern

The meaning of a pattern is made between the pattern and whoever reads it. So I ask three things: what is the data, who reads it, and how do people take part? Here they are, asked three times.

In the zine the marks were placed for the reader. Here you place them yourself.

Why this matters to people who design

Designers ship more and more interfaces that read data for people: a score, a summary, a generated scenario. Every such reading carries someone's idea of what counts. What stays with the person is their answer to the reading: placing the mark, disagreeing with it. That place can be designed.

I am a designer, and this is part of my master's research into how patterns come to carry meaning, and who gets to say what they mean.

How your text is made

Every step on every question has one sentence, written in advance. Your scenario text is those sentences put together by three rules.

  1. Today. The four steps you marked for today are joined into one paragraph: “You see the Netherlands today as a country where …”.
  2. Prediction. Only the questions where your prediction differs from today go into the second paragraph, with how soon. If nothing moved, it says so.
  3. Price. The question you moved farthest adds one line on what that move costs. Two questions tied for farthest add a line each.

Data policy

What the booth keeps:

  • Your marks: today, your prediction, and the time frame.
  • Your answer to “Is this where you want it to go?”.
  • Your consent answer: whether your marks and words may be shown in the research publication.
  • If you gave them: your one line of reasoning, and how long you have lived in the Netherlands, and how much you work with digital technology.
  • If you left one: your email address. It is kept apart from your answers and used only to send you the results and news about this research.

The booth never keeps your name. To have your email removed, reply to any email you get from the booth.

Disclaimer

  • The scenario text is put together from sentences written in advance. It is something to think with, not a forecast and not advice.
  • The questions simplify. Five steps between two answers cannot hold a whole country.
  • The cards are not a survey of the Netherlands. They show the people who came by.
  • Cards marked “invented” are made up, so the page is not empty before the booth has visitors.

Terms of use

  • Taking part is free and voluntary. No money changes hands; the receipt is a keepsake.
  • By donating you agree that your marks are shown here among the others, without your name. Whether they appear in the research publication is your consent answer.
  • Please keep names and contact details out of your line of reasoning.
  • You can change your mind. Tell the booth your card number, which is on your receipt, and your card is removed.
